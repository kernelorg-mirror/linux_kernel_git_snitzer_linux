NFSD on the Xsight E1 · 16 KiB pages · build 463 · 10 October 2026

16 KiB pages and 5440-byte receive buffers: 41 GB/s of NFS WRITEs in 21 MiB of the E1's 32 MiB L3 cache.

An NFS server on the E1 now takes 41 GB/s of durable WRITEs from one client: sixteen connections over one port of the Xsight card, copied once on arrival and written direct to six striped NVMe drives. The wire itself carries 48 GB/s to a receiver that stores nothing. Two days earlier the same copy carried 25.4. With the card's receive buffers cut to 5440 bytes it does so in 21 MiB of receive region, inside the CPU's 32 MiB cache, and drops no frame. The 41 is the better of two traced runs at each of two settings; the others were 36.6 and 38.3. What it took is summarized first, below, with the call with Xsight's engineers on 9 October that set its direction, then the days before it. The second half of this page is the work that came first: the receive region and its budget, tested on 4 KiB, 16 KiB and 64 KiB kernels.

What the two runs each say. The rate does not separate 5440 from a whole page: each has a run at 41 GB/s and a run at 37 to 38, and two runs of one setting differ by as much as the settings do. What Hammerspace brought to it. By 9 October there had been a week of runs on the card, and so the call could be about how a buffer lives and not about which features the card lacks. The measurements already said that the wire carries line rate when nothing has to be stored, that the copy stopped near 25 to 28 GB/s, that the client spent its time waiting on the server, that the receive memory was being held inside the 32 MiB cache with back pressure to the sender, and that the page pool's locking was one of the costs. Earlier requests to Xsight had been for features other cards have, such as header-data split and coalescing of received frames. With those figures in hand the question put to them was a better one: how does a buffer have to move for this hardware to perform?

What Xsight said. A buffer has to be used again while it is still in the cache, so its whole life, from the ring to the copy and back to the pool, must fit in the cache's footprint. The buffer freed last should be the one allocated next, as DPDK does. One burst of delay can push the buffers out of cache into a slower state that does not recover, so try a load paced at the source and raised in steps. Flows reach the rings by the hash of their addresses and ports, so an even spread needs many more flows than rings, and is read from the per-ring counters as differences. More rings, or deeper ones, is more memory posted.

The Linux changes it took

1

A stacked device takes its drives' memory alignmentblock

MD raid0 asked for memory aligned to 512 bytes where each drive under it asks for 4, so nfsd's copy could not be written direct on the stripe and went through the page cache: a second copy. With the change every copied WRITE is direct. Worth 25.4 to 27.7 GB/s by itself.

2

nfsd trades pages with the socket when a part-received WRITE changes handssunrpc

A WRITE that one thread began receiving and another finished had its pages freed and allocated again at each handover. They are now swapped. The page allocator left the profile.

3

A thread waits briefly for the rest of a WRITEsunrpc

With a short wait for the next piece (the linger), a 1 MiB WRITE is received in about 2 reads by one thread. It is a busy wait and costs about a fifth more server CPU per GB; how short it can be is not settled.

4

Several nfsd thread pools on one NUMA nodesunrpc

All 512 threads took ready connections from one queue. Four pools give four queues. Its own worth has not been measured: the load that showed the contention had 64 connections.

5

The page pool reuses a region's pages last in, first outpage_pool

A pool bound to the region recycled through a first-in, first-out ring, so the page handed to the card next was the one idle longest and least likely to be in cache. It is now a stack. The count of pages turned away from a full ring went to 0 with the smaller buffers.

6

The region can be resized, and loaned pages return in batchespage_pool · sunrpc

A 32768-page region replaced the 6144-page one, which left no room for deeper rings. Loaned pages are returned to their pool a batch at a time. Neither is on the copy's path. The first version of the batching overran a 16-entry array on the stack and crashed the server in a loan run on 9 October; it is fixed, with a unit test and a build-time check, and the loan run has not been repeated yet.

The copy is as fast as the region in use is small. Once connections are no longer waiting, what limits the rate is the copy itself. Correction, 9 October, build 459: on the six-drive stripe nothing was written direct, copy or loan.

The stripe (MD raid0) reports that it needs memory aligned to 512 bytes, where each drive in it needs 4. A copied WRITE's data starts just after the RPC header, at an offset that is a multiple of 4 and not of 512, so nfsd writes it through the page cache instead of direct. A loan was going through the page cache already, because these drives need whole pages. Every fast figure on this page from the stripe is therefore: copy = two copies (out of the receive pages, then into the page cache), loan = one copy (into the page cache), and both written back, not direct. An earlier version of this section called the copy a direct write. On one drive it was; on the stripe it was not, and that was not checked until Mike asked.

This is a finding in its own right: a software stripe takes direct I/O away from nfsd's copy, and would take it from a loan too on drives that accept arbitrary pieces. The only direct writes on the stripe were through the NVMe/TCP stand-in: copy 24.8 GB/s, loan about 20.

Two kinds of storage, two right answers. One budget holds for both.

The words

What each term means here

Earlier documents used some of these loosely, and two of them for the same thing. This is how they are used now, in the code and the documents alike.

Loan
nfsd hands the file system the network card's own receive pages, holding the data just as it arrived. Nothing is copied. The pages are not free again until the write has finished.
Copy
nfsd copies the WRITE's data out of the receive pages into its own buffer, and the receive pages go straight back to the card.
Direct write
The file system sends memory to the device as it is. Nothing passes through the page cache.
Region
A fixed, small block of memory that named network devices must receive into. It is small so that the place packets land can stay in the CPU's cache, which is what makes copying out of it cheap. Being fixed, it can run out.
Budget
The configured limit, in pages, on how much of the region loans may hold at once. A connection whose next WRITE does not fit waits, and TCP slows its sender. A budget of 0 means copy. Set per host or per network namespace.
Credit
What one WRITE draws against the budget, and gives back when it ends. Only that. The limit itself is never called credit any more.
Held receive buffer
Each connection's TCP receive buffer fixed at a small size and not left to grow. It limits how much data nfsd has not read yet is sitting in the region.

How it comes together

The storage decides which path is right

A loaned WRITE reaches the file system as one piece per network packet, each ending part-way through a page. Whether that can be written direct is a property of the storage, and it splits the world in two.

Storage that needs whole pages

NVMe without SGLs

This is what tardis1 has. It cannot take a loan as a direct write, so a loan falls back to the page cache: the copy comes back, later, and the receive pages are held longer.

Right answer: copy on arrival. nfsd's own buffer is laid out in whole pages and is written direct. The region stays nearly empty.

region · budget 0 · receive buffer held

Storage that takes arbitrary pieces

NVMe with SGLs

A loan can be written direct as it stands, with no copy anywhere, provided the card puts the data on a 4-byte boundary. The price is that region pages are held until the device finishes.

Right answer: loan, under a budget. The budget is what stops loans from using the region up.

region · budget set · receive buffer held

One mechanism serves both. The region bounds where packets land. The budget says how much of it loans may hold, and at 0 turns every WRITE into a copy. The held receive buffer keeps unread data from filling the region in either case. The test stand-ins are nvme-loop for the first kind and NVMe over TCP for the second.

The 4-byte placement is a card setting, and the test rig now has it too.

By default a frame's data lands 2 bytes off a 4-byte boundary, which NVMe refuses as direct I/O. On the Xsight card that is fixed by loading its driver with rx_ip_align=2. The same misplacement shows up on a plain veth pair with no Xsight hardware: 0 of 512 loaned WRITEs went direct. veth was given the same setting for testing, and then 512 of 512 did. There is no general way to ask a network device for this placement; a design for one is written up in the tree and is not expected to be accepted upstream.

A loan was charged per packet fragment, not per region pagefixed

Δ

A waiting connection could block every other one for goodfixed

A connection refused budget waits its turn. If TCP rebuilt its queued data in ordinary memory meanwhile, it read on without asking again and stayed at the head of the queue, and every connection behind it waited on a release that never came. Seen once in a plan run and reproduced at about one in 34 repetitions of the tightest cell. With the fix, 80 of 80 repetitions finished, including one where the same event occurred. Δ

A record whose first short pieces had been merged into one page, and which then had to be copied, had that page's bytes subtracted twice. The receive failed with a kernel warning. Seen once; the fix is verified by a unit test that drives exactly that sequence, and has not been caught again at runtime. Not specific to 64 KiB.

Δ

What the 4 KiB and 16 KiB runs found

Twelve problems, in the kernel, two test devices and the harness

Each explains a change now in the tree. The ones that matter beyond those hosts:

Δ

A region with no budget could hang the serverfixed

Sixteen connections each part-way through a 1 MiB WRITE held the whole region between them, so none could finish. A budget of 0 now means copy in every case, including when no budget is set anywhere. This is also what makes "region, budget 0" a setup that needs no further configuration.

Δ

A misaligned write was cut in the wrong place on pages larger than 4 KiBfixed, then reworked

nfsd and LOCALIO write a misaligned write's middle direct and its ends buffered. The cut was made at the storage's 4096-byte alignment, which is a page boundary only on 4 KiB pages. On 16 KiB each write then read back the pages it shared with its neighbours: 0.99 reads per WRITE, 0.003 once a misaligned end was cut at a page boundary. tardis1 later found the remaining case, an end that is aligned to the device but not to the page (above).

Δ

Two block-layer test devices were wrongupstream fixes

null_blk lost data when its block size was smaller than the page. dm-delay rounded short delays up to a timer tick, so the harness's 1 ms storage delay was 10 ms at 100 Hz. Every throughput figure taken before that fix was set by the tick. Both stand on their own as fixes.

i

A direct write to a growing file is slow on XFS, loaned or copiedfinding

Eight nfsd threads creating one file queue behind one another: about 350 MB/s, against 1600 to 2100 for the same writes to a file that already has its size. This looked like loans being slow until the copy was shown to be just as slow. tardis1 traced the wait itself (above).

i

The held receive buffer costs a single connection up to a thirdfinding

The same copied cell ran at about 1340 MB/s with the buffer held at 128 KiB, 1650 at 256 KiB and 2000 with it not held. Not measured with several connections or on hardware.

Δ

The budget-change cell changed the budget after its load had finishedharness · 4 KiB

n-reconf changes a namespace's budget at 6, 12 and 18 seconds, but its load was sized in data. Once the storage delay was honest, that load finished in about 4 seconds, so on both hosts the budget changed with nothing in flight, and the cell passed without testing the two lost-wake fixes it exists for. It now runs ten verified passes, about 40 seconds. Re-run at 4 KiB, records were in flight and waiting at every change and the waiters drained each time. Re-run at 16 KiB, the load ran through all three changes and the cell ended with no waiters and its data verified.

Δ

The plan could not compare a copy with a loan, and could pass with nothing runharness

Its copy cells differed from its loan cells in three things at once. Every loan cell now has a twin that copies, the plan runs every budget phase on both kinds of storage, each cell records how its WRITEs were issued, and a stage fails on any verify failure, hang or missing table.

The test plan ran on virtual network devices with memory for storage. A copy there is nearly free, which is why copy and loan come out level in it. On the Xsight card it is not free: during a copy at 25 GB/s half of the server's cycles were copying (two copies on the stripe, as it turned out), and the loan, with one, carried a fifth more on less than half the CPU. With the copy written direct, one copy each, the loan carries 9% more and costs the server more CPU per GB, 0.54 against 0.48; and with a 1 MiB receive buffer the copy carries 35.9 GB/s, more than the loan (the second section). The on-die receive copy design rests on a region kept in cache so that the copy is cheap. That now shows directly: with connections no longer waiting, a 1 MiB WRITE's copy takes 224 µs with 24 MiB of the region in use and 357 µs with 48 MiB, and the rate follows. Whether the data was in cache was not measured, only what the copy took.

Still unexplained on this rig: a loan written direct is about 6% slower than the same loan written through the page cache when the storage has a delay, with the difference inside the one request to the device.
