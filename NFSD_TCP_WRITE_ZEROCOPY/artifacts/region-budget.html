NFSD on the Xsight E1 · 16 KiB pages · build 464 · 11 October 2026

16 KiB pages, 8192-byte receive buffers and an MTU of 7792: 44 GB/s of NFS WRITEs inside the E1's 32 MiB L3 cache.

An NFS server on the E1 now takes 44 GB/s of durable WRITEs from one client: sixteen connections over one port of the Xsight card, copied once on arrival and written direct to six striped NVMe drives. That is 92% of what the wire carries to a receiver that stores nothing (48 GB/s), and three days earlier the same copy carried 25.4. The receive memory in use stays at 24 to 25 MiB, inside the CPU's 32 MiB cache. Three settings do it together: the card's receive buffers cut to 8192 bytes with the MTU lowered so that each frame fills one, each connection's receive buffer held at 1 MiB, and every connection placed on a receive ring so that each ring carries a quarter. On build 464, 21 runs of that setting gave 43.4 to 44.3; the highest single run of the day was 44.6. The configuration that is pinned now is first, below, then build 464: more than one thread receiving and copying on a connection, a larger receive window, and pacing at the client all reach the same ceiling, and the client is not what holds it. Then what it took to get here, with the call with Xsight's engineers on 9 October that set its direction, then the loan, made ready for NVMe drives with SGLs, with a setting the kernel now measures for itself, then what was tried before it and did not help, then the days before it. The second half of this page is the work that came first: the receive region and its budget, tested on 4 KiB, 16 KiB and 64 KiB kernels.

Build 464 holds 44 GB/s, and three ways of draining a connection faster reach the same ceiling

Build 464 repeats the best setting at 43.4 to 44.3 GB/s over 21 runs. The client is already putting 355 Gbit/s on the wire, so about 3.5 GB/s is all that is left.

The setting is the one from 10 October: 8192-byte receive buffers at an MTU of 7792, four rings of 256, a 1 MiB held buffer, sixteen placed connections. Build 464 has the whole stack built in, and the kernel now measures what a connection's receive buffer holds and sets the window from it (487 KiB under a 1 MiB buffer here). The first runs gave 43.0 and 42.5. Pinning all 512 nfsd threads to the CPUs away from the rings, where only the first 128 had been pinned, coincides with the rise to 44; that was not run as its own comparison.

Then the question was what limits a connection. A profile showed the copy being made with the connection's socket locked, and the client waiting on the server's window 96% of the time. Three changes went after that, and a look at the client followed.

More than one thread receiving and copying on a connection

nfsd reads a connection with one thread at a time, and that thread copies each WRITE's data out of the receive pages while it holds the socket. Two changes take the copy out of the way, both off unless set:

1

The copy made with the socket unlockedsunrpc

The thread takes its own reference to the received data and drops the socket lock while it copies, so the network stack can queue what arrives meanwhile.

2

Claim, then copy: svc_tcp_rx_copy_threadssunrpc

The thread only claims a WRITE's data while it reads the socket, which returns the window to the client at once, and copies afterwards. With 2 or more it gives the connection up before it copies, so another thread reads the next WRITE while the first is still copying.

It works and it does not raise the rate. The window is shut less, as intended, and the rate stays where it was. Retransmits rise about ten times, which is not yet explained. Data written with 1, 2 and 8 threads was read back and checksummed without error. Claiming stays off on one port. Six runs of each, 0 and 2 threads by turns with nothing else varied, gave 43.9 and 44.2 GB/s on average, a difference smaller than two runs of one setting show.

The retransmits are frames the card loses and does not count. Every packet of one run of each was accounted for. With two threads the client put 234,702 more frames on the wire than the card's rings report as forwarded or dropped, and retransmitted 240,733; without claiming the figures are 5,153 and 10,492. Nothing in the server's network stack drops them. A thread that claims returns the window sooner, the server sends two thirds more ACKs, and the client sends closer together; the card then loses frames ahead of its rings. So claiming does not mishandle TCP, and a setting's loss has to be read from the client's retransmits, since the card's ring drops miss most of it. Where those frames go is a question for Xsight.

Three earlier runs that copied as the socket was read had hundreds of retransmission timeouts and carried 34 to 40 GB/s. Twenty further runs of that kind, watched every second, did not show another, so what happens in such a run is not known.

Pacing at the client: fq against fq_codel

The client's port queues with fq_codel, which is the default and does not pace: a connection sends whatever its windows allow in one burst. fq spreads the same data out at TCP's pacing rate. If bursts are what the rings drop, pacing should let a larger window through.

Pacing removes most of the loss and none of the ceiling. Drops and retransmits fall to between a tenth and two fifths at every size. The rate is lower at 1 MiB and 1.5 MiB and level at 2 MiB, because a paced sender spends longer waiting on the window.

What it means. The same 44 GB/s is reached three ways: with a window small enough that the sender waits on it, with a larger one that lets it lose frames, and with a larger one paced in between. So the limit is neither the window, the loss nor the bursts alone, and the port is within 8% of full. For a deployment it means the client needs nothing changed: the default fq_codel with a 1 MiB held buffer is the best pairing and the one that stays inside the cache. A client that does pace with fq gives up about 2 GB/s at 1 MiB, and gets it back with a 2 MiB held buffer at the cost of 35 MiB of receive memory, which is more than the L3 cache.

10 October: 8192-byte receive buffers with the MTU at 7792, a 1 MiB held buffer and every connection placed on a ring. 43.2 and 43.3 GB/s, in 24 to 26 MiB of the 32 MiB cache.

Denser buffers keep more data in the cache. The same rings and the same held buffer hold more payload in less memory, so the copy finds its data in cache more often: its share of the server's cycles is 25% at 8192 bytes against 29% at 5440. It is the one change that raised the rate and lowered the memory together. A fifth more frames arrive for the same data, and the rings took them with 9 and 7 dropped.

One buffer to a frame also matters for what comes next. With two buffers to a frame the second is not placed on a 4-byte boundary, which a direct write of loaned pages needs. With one, every piece is placed. Only the server's MTU is lowered; the client's stays at 9000.

Around the buffers, three settings. A 1 MiB held receive buffer is worth about 1.7 GB/s over 685 KiB: more of a WRITE has arrived when a thread reads it. Rings of 256 frames drop next to nothing where rings of 128 dropped a hundred thousand to nine hundred thousand frames a run. And placing every connection on a ring made runs repeat: two runs of a setting are within about 1 GB/s, where the same setting had given 41.0 and 36.6.

What the peak depends on

Four things have to hold at once. Take any one away and the rate falls or stops repeating.

1

One copy, written direct

nfsd copies a WRITE out of the receive pages once and the file system sends that buffer to the drives as it is. A second copy through the page cache held the same load to 25 GB/s.

2

A connection that never waits for data

A receive buffer deep enough to hold a whole WRITE, and one thread that stays with the WRITE until it is all in. With a shallow buffer each connection sat idle for more than half the time.

3

Receive memory that stays inside the L3 cache

Receive buffers a frame fills (8192 bytes at an MTU of 7792), rings sized in frames, and pages reused last in, first out. Copying a WRITE takes 60% longer when the memory in use grows from 24 MiB to 48 MiB; the best setting uses 24 to 26.

4

Every flow placed, so each ring carries a quarter

Each ring is served by one CPU, so the busiest ring is the first thing to run out. Left to the card's hash, sixteen connections have put 36% to 42% of the traffic on one ring and as little as 6% on another, differently at every mount. On build 462 the slowest of three like runs was the one with a ring at twice its share. The harness now reads each connection's hash and sets the card's RSS table so that four connections land on each ring. Under NFS the rings went from 23, 21, 36 and 20% to 25.0, 25.0, 25.4 and 24.6% (below), and two runs of one setting now come within about 1 GB/s of each other where they differed by more than 4.

What Hammerspace brought to it. By 9 October there had been a week of runs on the card, and so the call could be about how a buffer lives and not about which features the card lacks. The measurements already said that the wire carries line rate when nothing has to be stored, that the copy stopped near 25 to 28 GB/s, that the client spent its time waiting on the server, that the receive memory was being held inside the 32 MiB cache with back pressure to the sender, and that the page pool's locking was one of the costs. Earlier requests to Xsight had been for features other cards have, such as header-data split and coalescing of received frames. With those figures in hand the question put to them was a better one: how does a buffer have to move for this hardware to perform?

What Xsight said. A buffer has to be used again while it is still in the cache, so its whole life, from the ring to the copy and back to the pool, must fit in the cache's footprint. The buffer freed last should be the one allocated next, as DPDK does. One burst of delay can push the buffers out of cache into a slower state that does not recover, so try a load paced at the source and raised in steps. Flows reach the rings by the hash of their addresses and ports, so an even spread needs many more flows than rings, and is read from the per-ring counters as differences. More rings, or deeper ones, is more memory posted.

One port of the card, a separate client machine. Kernel 7.1.13-21.hs.463.loanpages_pool.el9.aarch64+16k and kmod-xsight 2.0.1-6.

Only the server's MTU is lowered. The client keeps 9000, and nothing on the client changes.

Why that works. When a TCP connection opens, each end tells the other the largest segment it will accept, worked out from its own MTU. The server now says 7752 bytes. A sender never exceeds what the receiver asked for, so the client cuts its data into segments the server's buffers hold, whatever its own MTU is. Measured on the client during a run: its connections to the server use a segment size of 7740 bytes (7752 less the 12 bytes of TCP timestamps) while its own port still reports an MTU of 9000.

Why it is safe. The limit is agreed for each connection, by the two ends, before any data moves, so there is nothing to fragment and nothing to discover along the path. In the other direction the server sends frames of at most 7792 bytes, which a 9000-byte port accepts. Nothing between the two hosts needs changing, and the client's other traffic, to other servers, still uses 9000.

The one limit. This protects TCP only. A single packet larger than 7792 bytes that is not TCP, such as a large ping or a large UDP datagram, has no such agreement and does not get through: an 8500-byte ping from the client to the server goes unanswered, where a 7792-byte one is answered. NFS here runs over TCP, including the mount, so it is not affected.

MD raid0 asked for memory aligned to 512 bytes where each drive under it asks for 4, so nfsd's copy could not be written direct on the stripe and went through the page cache: a second copy. With the change every copied WRITE is direct. Worth 25.4 to 27.7 GB/s by itself.

A WRITE that one thread began receiving and another finished had its pages freed and allocated again at each handover. They are now swapped. The page allocator left the profile.

A 1 MiB WRITE arrives as about 118 jumbo frames, which the network stack hands up a burst at a time. nfsd read whatever had arrived and, if the WRITE was not complete, parked it on the connection and went on to other work; the next burst woke some thread to read the next piece.

What the pieces cost. Every extra read is a thread woken and a WRITE handed over. In between, the rest of the WRITE sits unread in the connection's receive queue, in pages that came off the rings, and those pages cannot go back to the pool until some thread gets to them. So the receive memory in use is larger, the rings are refilled with pages that have been idle longer, and the connection's window stays shut to the client. With the 256 KiB buffer a connection was being read for only 44% of the time: for 38% it was waiting for data and for 18% for a thread. A piece is about 13 µs in coming, so a wait far shorter than a wake-up keeps one thread on the WRITE until it is whole.

With the wait and the other changes loaded together, frames dropped at the rings fell from 251 thousand to about 3 thousand in the same run on rings of 4 x 256; how much of that is the wait alone has not been measured. It is a busy wait and costs about a fifth more server CPU per GB (0.52 against 0.43 CPU-seconds), and how short it can be is not settled.

4

Everything above is copy mode, which is the right answer for the drives tardis1 has today: they need whole pages. Sample NVMe drives that support SGLs are on their way, and those can take a WRITE's network pages as they arrived, with no copy at all. NVMe over TCP has been their stand-in throughout, so that the whole path is in place when they come. This is where it stands.

On 8192-byte buffers with the MTU at 7792, all 2.5 million loaned WRITEs of a run were written direct. None fell back to the page cache.

What a direct write of loaned pages needs. Each piece of a WRITE handed to the storage must start on a 4-byte boundary. nfsd checks every piece, and one that fails sends the whole WRITE through the page cache: the copy comes back, later. The charge for a loan: the kernel now learns it

A budget limits how much receive memory loans may hold. Each WRITE is charged, when it starts, for the pages it is expected to hold, and that estimate was a setting someone had to get right: svc_tcp_rx_credit_factor. The right value depends on how the card cuts its pages into buffers and on how the frames of different connections fall into them. With two buffers to a page, a 1 MiB WRITE holds 68 pages if its frames pair up and up to 136 if each shares its page with another connection's.

That is the one pattern in all five. A deeper buffer, more rings and more connections each put more data in receive memory, which ends some of the waiting and pushes the memory past the 32 MiB cache. Sixteen connections come out at 41 to 42 GB/s either way.

The first half of the project · 4 KiB, then 16 KiB, then 64 KiB on the E1

Two kinds of storage, two right answers. One budget holds for both.

NFSD can hand a WRITE's network receive pages straight to the file system, or copy the data out of them first. Which is right depends on what the storage can take. A fixed receive region with a budget makes both safe. On 4 KiB, 16 KiB and now 64 KiB kernels the whole test plan passes on both kinds of storage. On the test rigs copying and loaning come out level in speed. Those rigs are virtual machines on a MacBook Pro for two of the three page sizes: a VMware Fusion guest for 4 KiB and a UTM guest for 16 KiB. The words

What each term means here

Earlier documents used some of these loosely, and two of them for the same thing. This is how they are used now, in the code and the documents alike.

Loan
nfsd hands the file system the network card's own receive pages, holding the data just as it arrived. Nothing is copied. The pages are not free again until the write has finished.
Copy
nfsd copies the WRITE's data out of the receive pages into its own buffer, and the receive pages go straight back to the card.
Direct write
The file system sends memory to the device as it is. Nothing passes through the page cache.
Region
A fixed, small block of memory that named network devices must receive into. It is small so that the place packets land can stay in the CPU's cache, which is what makes copying out of it cheap. Being fixed, it can run out.
Budget
The configured limit, in pages, on how much of the region loans may hold at once. A connection whose next WRITE does not fit waits, and TCP slows its sender. A budget of 0 means copy. Set per host or per network namespace.
Credit
What one WRITE draws against the budget, and gives back when it ends. Only that. The limit itself is never called credit any more.
Held receive buffer
Each connection's TCP receive buffer fixed at a small size and not left to grow. It limits how much data nfsd has not read yet is sitting in the region.

How it comes together

The storage decides which path is right

A loaned WRITE reaches the file system as one piece per network packet, each ending part-way through a page. Whether that can be written direct is a property of the storage, and it splits the world in two.

Storage that needs whole pages

NVMe without SGLs

This is what tardis1 has. It cannot take a loan as a direct write, so a loan falls back to the page cache: the copy comes back, later, and the receive pages are held longer.

Right answer: copy on arrival. nfsd's own buffer is laid out in whole pages and is written direct. The region stays nearly empty.

region · budget 0 · receive buffer held

Storage that takes arbitrary pieces

NVMe with SGLs

A loan can be written direct as it stands, with no copy anywhere, provided the card puts the data on a 4-byte boundary. The price is that region pages are held until the device finishes.

Right answer: loan, under a budget. The budget is what stops loans from using the region up.

region · budget set · receive buffer held

One mechanism serves both. The region bounds where packets land. The budget says how much of it loans may hold, and at 0 turns every WRITE into a copy. The held receive buffer keeps unread data from filling the region in either case. The test stand-ins are nvme-loop for the first kind and NVMe over TCP for the second.

The 4-byte placement is a card setting, and the test rig now has it too.

By default a frame's data lands 2 bytes off a 4-byte boundary, which NVMe refuses as direct I/O. On the Xsight card that is fixed by loading its driver with rx_ip_align=2. The same misplacement shows up on a plain veth pair with no Xsight hardware: 0 of 512 loaned WRITEs went direct. veth was given the same setting for testing, and then 512 of 512 did. There is no general way to ask a network device for this placement; a design for one is written up in the tree and is not expected to be accepted upstream.

What tardis1 found

Four kernel defects, two of them not about page size

Builds 454, 455 and 457 each stopped on one of these. All four are fixed in build 458.

Δ

A loan was charged per packet fragment, not per region pagefixed

On 64 KiB pages veth packs about seven jumbo frames into one page, so a 1 MiB WRITE holding 17 pages was charged about 117 against a credit of 25. Every loan was throttled to a quarter of the budget: 1000 MB/s where its copy ran at 2156. With pages counted once, the one-connection pairs are level and nothing overruns. Where a frame has a page to itself the charge is unchanged, which is from the code and not yet from a run.

Δ

A waiting connection could block every other one for goodfixed

A connection refused budget waits its turn. If TCP rebuilt its queued data in ordinary memory meanwhile, it read on without asking again and stayed at the head of the queue, and every connection behind it waited on a release that never came. Seen once in a plan run and reproduced at about one in 34 repetitions of the tightest cell. With the fix, 80 of 80 repetitions finished, including one where the same event occurred. Not specific to 64 KiB.

Δ

Falling back to a copy part-way through a record could count bytes twicefixed

A record whose first short pieces had been merged into one page, and which then had to be copied, had that page's bytes subtracted twice. The receive failed with a kernel warning. Seen once; the fix is verified by a unit test that drives exactly that sequence, and has not been caught again at runtime. Not specific to 64 KiB.

Δ

Two neighbouring misaligned writes in one page could fail one of themfixed

Through LOCALIO, one write's direct middle and its neighbour's buffered end could land in the same page; the direct write then could not drop the page from the cache and the neighbour's write returned an I/O error. The test did not notice at first. Cutting every write at page boundaries cured it but made writes aligned to the device, and not to a 64 KiB page, mostly buffered. The cut is per end again, and LOCALIO now finishes a direct write that shares a page before it returns: 12 of 12 focused runs clean where 6 of 6 failed. Only XFS was run.

A direct write that starts beyond the end of the file waits for every direct write in progress: 95 ms on average here, against 5 microseconds on a file that has its size. What the 4 KiB and 16 KiB runs found

Twelve problems, in the kernel, two test devices and the harness

Each explains a change now in the tree. The ones that matter beyond those hosts:

Δ

A region with no budget could hang the serverfixed

Sixteen connections each part-way through a 1 MiB WRITE held the whole region between them, so none could finish. A budget of 0 now means copy in every case, including when no budget is set anywhere. This is also what makes "region, budget 0" a setup that needs no further configuration.

Δ

A misaligned write was cut in the wrong place on pages larger than 4 KiBfixed, then reworked

nfsd and LOCALIO write a misaligned write's middle direct and its ends buffered. The cut was made at the storage's 4096-byte alignment, which is a page boundary only on 4 KiB pages. On 16 KiB each write then read back the pages it shared with its neighbours: 0.99 reads per WRITE, 0.003 once a misaligned end was cut at a page boundary. tardis1 later found the remaining case, an end that is aligned to the device but not to the page (above).

Δ

Two block-layer test devices were wrongupstream fixes

null_blk lost data when its block size was smaller than the page. dm-delay rounded short delays up to a timer tick, so the harness's 1 ms storage delay was 10 ms at 100 Hz. Every throughput figure taken before that fix was set by the tick. Both stand on their own as fixes.

i

A direct write to a growing file is slow on XFS, loaned or copiedfinding

Eight nfsd threads creating one file queue behind one another: about 350 MB/s, against 1600 to 2100 for the same writes to a file that already has its size. This looked like loans being slow until the copy was shown to be just as slow. tardis1 traced the wait itself (above).

i

The held receive buffer costs a single connection up to a thirdfinding

The same copied cell ran at about 1340 MB/s with the buffer held at 128 KiB, 1650 at 256 KiB and 2000 with it not held. Not measured with several connections or on hardware.

Δ

The budget-change cell changed the budget after its load had finishedharness · 4 KiB

n-reconf changes a namespace's budget at 6, 12 and 18 seconds, but its load was sized in data. Once the storage delay was honest, that load finished in about 4 seconds, so on both hosts the budget changed with nothing in flight, and the cell passed without testing the two lost-wake fixes it exists for. It now runs ten verified passes, about 40 seconds. Re-run at 4 KiB, records were in flight and waiting at every change and the waiters drained each time. Re-run at 16 KiB, the load ran through all three changes and the cell ended with no waiters and its data verified.

Δ

The plan could not compare a copy with a loan, and could pass with nothing runharness

Its copy cells differed from its loan cells in three things at once. Every loan cell now has a twin that copies, the plan runs every budget phase on both kinds of storage, each cell records how its WRITEs were issued, and a stage fails on any verify failure, hang or missing table.

a first sign

The test plan ran on virtual network devices with memory for storage. A copy there is nearly free, which is why copy and loan come out level in it. On the Xsight card it is not free: during a copy at 25 GB/s half of the server's cycles were copying (two copies on the stripe, as it turned out), and the loan, with one, carried a fifth more on less than half the CPU. With the copy written direct, one copy each, the loan carries 9% more and costs the server more CPU per GB, 0.54 against 0.48; and with a 1 MiB receive buffer the copy carries 35.9 GB/s, more than the loan (the second section). The on-die receive copy design rests on a region kept in cache so that the copy is cheap. That now shows directly: with connections no longer waiting, a 1 MiB WRITE's copy takes 224 µs with 24 MiB of the region in use and 357 µs with 48 MiB, and the rate follows. Whether the data was in cache was not measured, only what the copy took.

Still unexplained on this rig: a loan written direct is about 6% slower than the same loan written through the page cache when the storage has a delay, with the difference inside the one request to the device.
