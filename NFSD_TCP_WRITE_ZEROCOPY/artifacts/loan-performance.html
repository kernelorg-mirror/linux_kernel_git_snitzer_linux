NFSD on the Xsight E1 · 16 KiB pages · build 464 · 13 October 2026 · early findings

At 60.4 GB/s copy mode is at limit of memory. A loaned WRITE asks a fraction of it.

An NFS server on the E1 takes 60.4 GB/s of durable WRITEs over two ports and 47.4 over one by copying each WRITE once on arrival. That is as far as copying goes here: the machine's four DIMMs carry 166 GB/s in theory, a copy sends every byte through them twice, a bare copy benchmark reaches 62 to 68 GB/s, and two ports of NFS already ask of memory what that benchmark does. The card can deliver 99 GB/s. The way to it is not to copy, which is the loan, and a loan can only be written straight to storage by NVMe drives with SGLs. Those drives are on their way. This page is what was measured ahead of them on 13 October: what a loaned byte costs in memory, and what decides it.

Where copy mode ends

Pushed as far as it goes: the copy is the whole of the limit

The drives tardis1 has today need whole pages, so nfsd copies each WRITE out of the network's receive pages into a buffer of its own and writes that direct. Over six days that copy went from 25.4 GB/s to 47.4 on one port, which is 95% of what the wire carries with no NFS at all, and to 60.4 on two.

Nothing that adds parallelism around the copy moved two ports off 60 GB/s: eight receive rings in four layouts, more threads copying a connection, fewer thread wakeups, pacing at the client. The receive path was traced at that rate and holds nothing else large. The last 4% inside the copy is accounted for and was left alone, because every way to it would make the loan worse.

So the 40 GB/s between what copy mode carries and what the card delivers is not there to be tuned out. It is the copy.

The loan hands the file system the receive pages themselves. Nothing is copied. For that to reach the drives as a direct write, the drives must take data in arbitrary pieces, because each network frame's payload starts part-way into a page. NVMe drives with SGLs do. The drives in tardis1 do not, so a loan to them falls back to the page cache, which is a copy made later.

The loan, in outline

How a loaned WRITE moves, and the settings that shape it

The two paths differ in one step, and that step changes what has to be bounded.

Copy

  1. The card writes arriving frames into pages of the receive region.
  2. nfsd copies the WRITE's data out into its own buffer.
  3. The receive pages go straight back to the card.
  4. The file system writes nfsd's buffer to the drives.
receive pages held for microseconds · every byte through memory twice

Loan

  1. The card writes arriving frames into pages of the receive region.
  2. nfsd hands those same pages to the file system.
  3. The file system writes them to the drives as they are.
  4. Only then do the pages go back to the card.
no copy · receive pages held until the drive has finished

A copy frees the card's memory at once and pays for it in memory bandwidth. A loan pays nothing for a copy and holds the card's memory for as long as the storage takes. The card receives into a fixed, small region, so pages that are out on loan are pages the card cannot receive into. Left alone, sixteen connections part-way through a WRITE each could hold all of it between them and stop. The settings below are what prevent that, and they are the terms the findings are stated in.

Region
The fixed block of memory the card receives into: 32,768 pages of 16 KiB here, shared by both ports. It is kept small on purpose, so that what is in use at any moment can sit in the CPU's 32 MiB cache.
Region in use
How much of the region holds data right now: frames waiting in the rings, data the connections have received and nfsd has not yet read, and pages out on loan. This is the loan's footprint.
Budget
The limit on how much of the region loans may hold at once, in pages. A budget of 0 means copy.
WRITEs let in
The budget stated as a count: how many 1 MiB WRITEs may be in progress as loans at the same time. "16 WRITEs let in" is a budget of 2,150 pages; 8 is 1,142; 4 is 638. A connection whose next WRITE does not fit waits, and TCP slows its sender. With sixteen connections and 8 let in, about half of them are waiting at any moment.
Charge
What one WRITE is counted as holding against the budget, taken when it starts and given back when the storage has finished it. The kernel learns it from what WRITEs actually held; no WRITE in these runs held more than it was charged.
Held receive buffer
Each connection's TCP receive buffer, fixed at a set size and not left to grow: 1 MiB, 384 KiB or 256 KiB in these runs. It caps how much data nfsd has not read yet is sitting in the region for that connection. Sixteen connections at 1 MiB can hold 16 MiB before any loan is counted.
Rings
The card's receive rings for the port, four of them, each posted with 256 or 128 buffers of 8192 bytes. Buffers posted and waiting for frames are in the region too: 8 MiB at 256, 4 MiB at 128.
Requests to memory for each line
The measure used below. A line is 64 bytes, the unit the cache works in. The machine's interconnect counts every request its cache makes of the memory controller, which separates data that came from the DIMMs from data that was already in the cache. Divided by the lines of payload carried, it says what a byte of payload cost in memory bandwidth.
Stand-in
NVMe over TCP on tardis1 itself, in front of a file on one drive. It takes data in arbitrary pieces as an SGL drive would, so a loan can be written direct to it. It then passes the data through TCP to itself and receives it into buffers of its own, which a drive would not do.

An earlier reading of the limit had a loan bounded by memory as a copy is, on the reasoning that a byte still enters memory once and leaves once. These measurements say otherwise: both of those passes go through the cache, and what decides a loan's cost is how much received data is waiting for the drives, against the size of the cache. That is what the region, the budget and the held buffer exist to bound.

1

The loan's cost follows its footprint, with no loss of rate

From 76 MiB in use to 46, the requests to memory for each line fall from 4.07 to 1.86 while the rate stays at 11.7 GB/s. At the smallest setting the loan asks under two fifths of what its copy does, and less than a copy straight to the drives (2.17), though it is still carrying the stand-in's extra passage.

2

The copy gains nothing from the same change

Its footprint falls too, from 49 MiB to 37, and its cost stays near 5. What a copy pays for is its destination: nfsd's own buffers, far larger in all than the cache and outside the region, written out to memory and fetched back for the drive.

3

Too small a budget costs rate, not memory

With 4 WRITEs let in, the loan is under one request a line (0.93 and 0.80) and carries 8.4 and 7.5 GB/s where 8 let in carries 11.7. Sixteen connections are sharing four WRITEs' worth of budget and most are waiting. Eight is the smallest budget here that carries the stand-in's full rate.

4

The loan path is sound on this kernel

Every loaned WRITE in every cell was written direct, 4.5 million in all, and none held more than it was charged. The loan also carries about 13% more than its copy on the stand-in, with a hundredth of the client's retransmits at the larger settings.
