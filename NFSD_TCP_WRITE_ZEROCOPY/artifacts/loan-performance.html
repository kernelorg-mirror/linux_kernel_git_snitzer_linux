NFSD on the Xsight E1 · 16 KiB pages · builds 464 and 473 · 13 and 14 October 2026 · early findings

At 60.4 GB/s copy mode is at the limit of memory.A loaned WRITE asks a fraction of it.

An NFS server on the E1 takes 60.4 GB/s of durable WRITEs over two ports and 47.8 over one by copying each WRITE once on arrival. That is as far as copying goes here: the machine's four DIMMs carry 166 GB/s in theory, a copy sends every byte through them twice, a bare copy benchmark reaches 62 to 68 GB/s, and two ports of NFS already ask of memory what that benchmark does. The card can deliver 99 GB/s. The way to it is not to copy, which is the loan, and a loan can only be written straight to storage by NVMe drives with SGLs. Those drives are on their way. This page is what was measured ahead of them on 13 October: how many bytes have to pass through memory for each byte of payload a loan carries, and what decides it. On 14 October the same measurements began again on the 7.3 kernel, and so far they agree.

Where copy mode ends

Pushed as far as it goes: the copy is the whole of the limit

The drives tardis1 has today need whole pages, so nfsd copies each WRITE out of the network's receive pages into a buffer of its own and writes that direct. Over seven days that copy went from 25.4 GB/s to 47.8 on one port, which is 96% of what the wire carries with no NFS at all, and to 60.4 on two.

What the card delivers on two ports when nothing is copied99.6

Nothing that adds parallelism around the copy moved two ports off 60 GB/s: eight receive rings in four layouts, more threads copying a connection, fewer thread wakeups, pacing at the client. The receive path was traced at that rate and holds nothing else large. The last 4% inside the copy is accounted for and was left alone, because every way to it would make the loan worse.

So the 40 GB/s between what copy mode carries and what the card delivers is not there to be tuned out. It is the copy.

The loan hands the file system the receive pages themselves. Nothing is copied. For that to reach the drives as a direct write, the drives must take data in arbitrary pieces, because each network frame's payload starts part-way into a page. NVMe drives with SGLs do. The drives in tardis1 do not, so a loan to them falls back to the page cache, which is a copy made later.

The loan, in outline

How a loaned WRITE moves, and the settings that shape it

The two paths differ in one step, and that step changes what has to be bounded.

Copy

  1. The card writes arriving frames into pages of the receive region.
  2. nfsd copies the WRITE's data out into its own buffer.
  3. The receive pages go straight back to the card.
  4. The file system writes nfsd's buffer to the drives.
receive pages held for microseconds · 2.17 bytes through memory for each byte of payload

Loan

  1. The card writes arriving frames into pages of the receive region.
  2. nfsd hands those same pages to the file system.
  3. The file system writes them to the drives as they are.
  4. Only then do the pages go back to the card.
no copy · receive pages held until the drive has finished

A copy frees the card's memory at once and pays for it in memory bandwidth. A loan pays nothing for a copy and holds the card's memory for as long as the storage takes. The card receives into a fixed, small region, so pages that are out on loan are pages the card cannot receive into. Left alone, sixteen connections part-way through a WRITE each could hold all of it between them and stop. The settings below are what prevent that, and they are the terms the findings are stated in.

Region
The fixed block of memory the card receives into: 32,768 pages of 16 KiB here, shared by both ports. It is kept small on purpose, so that what is in use at any moment can sit in the CPU's 32 MiB cache.
Footprint
How much of the region a load keeps in play: the buffers posted in its port's rings, the data its connections have received and nfsd has not yet read, and the pages out on loan. The card writes through all of it, so this is what has to fit in the cache.
Budget
The limit on how much of the region loans may hold at once, in pages. A budget of 0 means copy.
WRITEs let in
The budget stated as a count: how many 1 MiB WRITEs may be in progress as loans at the same time. "16 WRITEs let in" is a budget of 2,150 pages; 8 is 1,142; 4 is 638. A connection whose next WRITE does not fit waits, and TCP slows its sender. With sixteen connections and 8 let in, about half of them are waiting at any moment.
Charge
What one WRITE is counted as holding against the budget, taken when it starts and given back when the storage has finished it. The kernel learns it from what WRITEs actually held; no WRITE in these runs held more than it was charged.
Held receive buffer
Each connection's TCP receive buffer, fixed at a set size and not left to grow: 1 MiB, 384 KiB or 256 KiB in these runs. It caps how much data nfsd has not read yet is sitting in the region for that connection. Sixteen connections at 1 MiB can have 16 MiB of the region taken up by data waiting to be read. That is on top of the pages out on loan: the budget limits loans and this limits unread data, and the two add.
Rings
The card's receive rings for the port, four of them, each posted with 256 or 128 buffers of 8192 bytes. Buffers posted and waiting for frames are in the region too: 8 MiB at 256, 4 MiB at 128.
Bytes through memory for each byte of payload
The measure used below. The machine's interconnect counts every time its cache has to fetch from the DIMMs or write out to them, which separates data that came from memory from data that was already in the cache. Divided by the payload carried, it says how much of the memory's bandwidth each byte of payload used. A figure of 2 means two bytes moved through the DIMMs for every byte the server took in. The DIMMs move 166 GB/s at most, so the figure times the rate has to stay under that.
Stand-in
NVMe over TCP on tardis1 itself, in front of a file on one drive. It takes data in arbitrary pieces as an SGL drive would, so a loan can be written direct to it. It then passes the data through TCP to itself and receives it into buffers of its own, which a drive would not do.

Both passes of a loaned byte go through the cache. What decides its cost is how much received data is waiting for the drives, against the size of the cache. That is what the region, the budget and the held buffer exist to bound, and it is why a loan is not held to the 60 GB/s that a copy is: at 2.17 bytes through memory for each byte of payload, 60.4 GB/s needs about 131 GB/s of the DIMMs' 166. At 0.1 the DIMMs are hardly in it.

The loan's cost follows its footprint, with no loss of rate

From about 37 MiB held to 18, the bytes through memory for each byte of payload fall from 4.07 to 1.34 while the rate stays at 11.4 to 11.7 GB/s. At 18 MiB the loan uses under a third of what its copy does, and less than a copy straight to the drives (2.17), though it is still carrying the stand-in's extra passage.

2

It levels off near 1.3, and what is left is the stand-in's

With rings of 64 the figure is 1.36, 1.34 and 1.32 as the footprint goes from 18.5 MiB to 16.8. It has stopped falling, and all of that is well inside the 32 MiB cache. What remains is the stand-in passing the data through TCP to itself and into buffers of its own, which are ordinary memory outside the region and which a drive would not have. For a drive that takes the pages itself, the first finding's 0.1 is what a footprint of this size points at.

3

The copy gains nothing from the same change

A copy holds almost nothing in the region beyond its rings, 10 MiB falling to 4, and still uses 4.7 to 5.75 bytes of memory for each byte of payload. What a copy pays for is its destination: nfsd's own buffers, far larger in all than the cache and outside the region, written out to memory and fetched back for the drive.

4

The settings have edges, and they are now known for this stand-in

Fewer than 8 WRITEs let in costs rate: 6 gives up a tenth and 4 a third, because sixteen connections are sharing too little budget and most are waiting. A held buffer of 128 KiB gives up about 7%; 192 KiB does not. Rings of 64 reach the floor and drop 7 to 65 thousand frames a cell doing it; rings of 128 drop a couple of hundred. The best setting that loses nothing is rings of 128, a 192 to 256 KiB held buffer and 8 WRITEs let in, at 1.7 to 1.9 and 22 to 23 MiB.

5

The loan path is sound on this kernel

Every loaned WRITE in every cell was written direct, 7.3 million in all, and none held more than it was charged. The loan also carries about 13% more than its copy on the stand-in.
